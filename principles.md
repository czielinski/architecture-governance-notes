# Principles

These are the questions I use to assess whether architecture governance helps teams make and implement decisions. The process should reflect the organisation's scale, risk and delegated authority.

## 1. Define which decisions need coordination

Changes to shared interfaces, operating responsibilities, material expenditure or agreed standards need a visible decision path. Routine choices within a team's mandate should not require the same review. Make the threshold explicit so that delivery partners know when to raise a proposal.

## 2. Separate recommendation from authority

An architect can prepare the options and recommend a course of action while a service owner, executive or forum holds the decision authority. Record who decides, who contributes and how a disagreement is escalated. Several disciplines may need to approve different aspects of the same proposal.

## 3. Make the path work in both directions

Teams need a way to raise significant changes and recurring problems. They also need a reliable route for receiving agreed standards and enterprise requirements. Named technical contacts and regular cross-team discussions can support both directions; a forum alone does not establish that communication.

## 4. Reuse decisions within their assumptions

Supported patterns reduce repeated analysis when their scope fits. Keep assumptions and review triggers visible so that a previous decision does not become a permanent answer to a different problem. Document the reason for a material exception.

## 5. Connect design to delivery and operation

A technically coherent design still needs implementation capacity, funding and a team able to run it. Resolve these responsibilities alongside the architecture, including dependencies on suppliers and shared platforms.

## 6. Review usefulness as well as speed

Track where decisions wait, whether the evidence is sufficient and whether teams can act on the result. Look at repeated exceptions, unresolved conditions and the consequences observed after implementation. A quick approval can still leave the underlying problem unresolved.
